I am writing to apply for the Industrus Engineering Graduate Program as a graduate civil engineer with practical experience in transport model quality assurance. I am completing a Master of Professional Engineering (Civil) at the University of Technology Sydney. During a 12-week internship on the Behavioural Analytics & Scenario Insights System (BASIS) project, I checked the traffic, public transport and cycling network maps of Sydney for errors, confirming that pathways were correct and properly connected, and reviewed the model’s code and metric calculations. One of these checks led to a network connection error being corrected before further scenario testing. Inconsistencies I found were documented and passed to the UNSW research team for review. A commitment to ethical conduct and the highest standards of professional accountability (3.1)

Transport model results are only reliable if the network behind them is correct. Errors such as a missing or wrongly connected link can distort travel time, delay and accessibility outcomes. While checking the Sydney traffic, public transport and cycling maps, I looked for pathways that were incorrect or not properly connected, and reviewed the related code and metric calculations. On the cycling network, I found a path segment that was not connected to the adjoining route at an intersection, which forced cyclists onto a longer detour and inflated the travel times reported for that area. I documented the issue with supporting evidence and sent it to the UNSW team responsible for review, rather than correcting it myself or assuming it was acceptable. The team confirmed the error and corrected the connection, and I rechecked the affected results to confirm they were consistent. At Palathra Constructions, I learned how work is checked against building standards and drawings, which reinforced why accurate records matter.

Demonstrated ability to effectively communicate both with other engineers and with stakeholders from different fields (3.2)

Because the people reviewing my findings were in a different research team, each issue had to make sense without me explaining it in person. I recorded what I observed, where in the network it occurred, the supporting evidence and why it mattered for the results, so the UNSW team could act on it without needing follow-up questions. As a result, the team was able to locate and resolve the issue without needing to ask me for further explanation. I also worked with both the UTS and UNSW teams, adjusting how much technical background I included depending on who was reading.

The ability to engage with a creative, innovative and proactive environment (3.3)

BASIS uses modelling and digital tools to test transport scenarios against measures such as travel time, delay, throughput, congestion, reliability, accessibility and mode share. I did not treat model runs as routine output. I considered whether the networks and calculations behind each measure would produce results reliable enough to support decisions. I suggested adding a routine connectivity check whenever a new map layer is introduced, and the team noted it for future scenario testing. This showed me how much innovation in engineering depends on trustworthy tools, and I want to work in an environment that encourages new approaches to that kind of problem.

Demonstrated ability to use and manage information (3.4)

My BASIS work involved several linked types of information: three network maps, model code, metric calculations and the issues I found. To keep this manageable, I recorded each issue in a consistent log noting the map layer, location, related code or calculation, supporting evidence and date, which meant the team could locate and verify each issue quickly. My Master’s studies have also developed my skills in technical reporting and engineering analysis.

Reflection on my internship

Before I began the internship on the BASIS project, I expected the placement to be similar to the site-based work I had done at Palathra Constructions — hands-on, physical, and focused on watching construction activity in real time. I thought a transport modelling placement would mean sitting through demonstrations of software rather than being responsible for output that other people relied on.

The reality was different. From early on I was given real Sydney network maps to check and real code and metric calculations to review, and the issues I found were treated as genuine findings that the UNSW team needed to act on, not as training exercises. I had not expected how much the accuracy of a single network connection could affect a whole set of results, or how much independent judgement was required to decide when something looked wrong enough to flag. I also had not expected to be documenting findings for a team I never met in person, which meant learning to write clearly enough that my evidence spoke for itself.

The most important lesson was the discipline of documenting and escalating rather than simply fixing what I noticed. Early in the internship it would have been easy to quietly correct an error in a map, but I learned that in a research setting, unverified changes are more dangerous than the error itself — every fix needs a record, evidence and a second set of eyes. This mattered because it taught me that quality assurance is as much about traceability as it is about catching mistakes, a principle I expect to carry into design checking and construction supervision later in my career. A second lesson came from balancing the internship with full-time study: setting aside fixed weekly blocks for map checks and providing regular updates to my supervisor kept me accountable and taught me to manage competing priorities without letting either suffer.

After this experience, I would describe my value proposition to an employer as a combination of methodical attention to detail and the ability to communicate technical findings clearly to people outside my immediate team. I can demonstrate this through the connectivity error I identified and documented on BASIS, which was corrected on the strength of the evidence I provided rather than requiring further explanation from me, and through the consistency I maintained leading a team of four on my undergraduate energy storage project.

The internship also shifted the type of role I am interested in. Before BASIS, I expected to move directly into construction site supervision, following on from my experience at Palathra Constructions. Having now worked on the modelling and quality-assurance side of a major infrastructure project, I am more drawn to roles that combine technical review with construction delivery — such as project engineering or quality assurance positions — where the same discipline of checking, documenting and communicating findings applies whether the "site" is a physical one or a digital network model.
